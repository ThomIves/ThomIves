NEOGOV Round 4 — Phil Dewing 30-Minute Prep

What this interview is

Thursday 2026-07-02, 1:00 PM Mountain, 30 minutes. Your fourth round conversation for the NEOGOV Revenue AI Architect role. Interviewer: Phil Dewing, VP of Sales at NEOGOV.

This is the "business partner" test. Nick vetted revenue-ops fluency in Round 2. Zach vetted application-stack fluency in Round 3. Phil is testing whether you can be a partner to his sellers — not an ivory-tower architect who ships things sellers won't use.

The stakes: if Phil trusts you, the role becomes real. If Phil doesn't, all the technical wins from prior rounds start eroding.

Why a VP of Sales is on this loop

A Sales VP interviewing an AI Architect is not unusual for a Revenue AI Architect role specifically. Phil is likely on the loop because:

  1. His sellers are the end users of whatever Revenue AI signals the role would produce. The Brief, the Seller Action Sheet, the CRO-facing scorecards — Phil's team consumes them.
  2. He owns adoption risk. A brilliant AI system nobody uses is worse than no system at all. Phil has veto power on tools that add work to a seller's day.
  3. He owns the business-outcome case internally. If the CEO or CFO asks "what did we get for this Revenue AI investment?" — Phil answers, not the AI architect.

That means Phil is measuring: would I want this person embedded with my sales leaders? Will they respect my sellers' time? Will they speak my language, or will I have to translate every conversation?

The good news — you have the exact right credential

Echo Global Logistics is the load-bearing story for Phil. Echo is a freight brokerage — one of the most brutally sales-driven businesses on the planet. You built AI for actual sellers there, at production scale, that they actually used. Not a demo. Not a POC. A real tool with real adoption in a real sales-driven company.

The Echo canonical phrasing (memorize the shape):

"I built two full-stack LLM-based AI Outlook Add-Ins at Echo Global Logistics — the freight brokerage. One on the sales-rep side for rapid rate-quote replies to customers. One on the carrier side that helped actual carriers find shipments they could ask to take at an offered price. Combined, 30,000+ daily requests across both. Per-request handling went from 5+ minutes to under 15 seconds. Both sides of the market got faster — sellers moved more deals, carriers filled more capacity."

Why this lands with Phil:

  • Both sides of the market — you know sales-tech has multiple stakeholders, not just the seller
  • Time compression at scale — 5+ minutes → 15 seconds × 30,000 daily = literal thousands of seller-hours reclaimed
  • Deployed in production, used daily — not a prototype, not a POC
  • Freight brokerage context — Phil will recognize this as a real sales-heavy business, not a startup demo

What Phil most likely cares about

In rough priority order:

1. Will your AI actually help my sellers hit their number?

Sales VPs measure everything in terms of quota attainment, deal velocity, forecast accuracy, and win rate. AI that doesn't move these needles isn't valuable to Phil regardless of how sophisticated it is.

Your ground: the Echo Outlook Add-Ins directly compressed the sales cycle (5+ min → 15s). That's deal velocity. Phil will hear that.

2. Will your AI be intrusive or additive to seller workflow?

Every seller has a tool graveyard — the sales-enablement platform their VP made them adopt in 2023 and hasn't been touched since. Phil is deeply skeptical of tools that add clicks to a seller's day.

Your ground: the Outlook Add-Ins ran INSIDE the seller's existing email flow — you delivered AI to where the seller already worked, not where they'd have to go. That's the right instinct.

3. How fast will the results show up?

Sales VPs think in quarters. If your AI takes 12 months to show adoption, Phil is 3 quarters into a bad forecast conversation with the CEO.

Your ground: iterative deployment with a small pilot group, measure the seller-hour impact within 30-60 days, then expand. The Echo pattern was this.

4. Will you respect his sellers' time in your architecture choices?

If your AI needs sellers to log 15 fields before it can produce anything useful, Phil will kill it. He wants AI that takes what's already in the CRM and infers signal — not AI that demands more input.

Your ground: the Microsoft Fabric AI Functions classifier at ABM AI takes free-text seller dispositions AS-IS and produces structured classification. That's the same posture — meet the seller where they are.

5. Can you communicate with non-technical stakeholders?

Sales VPs are frustrated by architects who can't explain what they're building in outcome terms. Phil is measuring whether he'd trust you in a board briefing or with a customer he's trying to close.

Your ground: you can absolutely speak in business terms. Just remember to.

The five most likely question shapes

Q1 — "Tell me about your experience with sales-facing AI"

Deploy the Echo Outlook Add-Ins story. ~30 seconds. Land it, then stop.

Q2 — "How would you think about the first 90 days if you joined?"

Answer shape:

"Not by proposing an architecture. By spending real time with your sellers — sitting in on their calls if I can, watching how they use Salesforce, understanding what they actually do all day. And by talking with you about what your commit conversations with the CEO sound like — where the forecast slips, where the signal is missing. Once I understand that, then I'd talk architecture."

That's exactly the right shape. Phil hears "this person will actually listen to my sellers before proposing anything."

Q3 — "What if my sellers push back on adoption?"

Answer shape:

"Then I'd want to understand why. Usually seller pushback on AI tools comes from one of three places: the tool adds work rather than removing it, the tool is wrong often enough to be untrustworthy, or the tool competes for attention with the actual selling conversation. All three are architecture problems, not seller problems. If they're pushing back, I've made an architectural mistake, and I'd rather learn that in week two than quarter two."

Signals: you take seller feedback seriously, you don't blame sellers for tool failures, you architect for adoption.

Q4 — "How do you measure whether the AI is working?"

Answer shape:

"Two layers. The narrow measure is what the AI itself is doing — precision, recall, cost per inference, latency. Those matter for engineering but not for you. The layer that matters for your seat is what happens downstream — deal velocity change, forecast accuracy delta, seller-hours saved, win-rate movement on the deals the AI touched vs. matched deals it didn't touch. I'd want to instrument both layers from day one so we can tell if the AI is technically working AND business-effective — those are different questions."

Signals: you know the difference between engineering metrics and business metrics.

Q5 — "How do you think about the AI vs. the seller?"

This is the fear-of-replacement question in disguise. Phil doesn't want AI that makes his sellers feel replaceable — his top performers have offers from competitors in their inbox.

Answer shape:

"AI works best when it hands sellers superpowers, not replacements. My job as the architect is to figure out what the seller does best that we should protect their time for, and what they do reluctantly that we should offload. At Echo, the sellers were spending 5+ minutes per email drafting rate quotes — nobody enjoys that work. The AI took it, gave them 15 seconds instead, and they used the reclaimed time to talk to customers. That's the shape."

Signals: you understand sellers as people, not as workflow endpoints.

Questions to ask Phil (have 3-4 ready)

Deploy 2-3 based on the energy in the room:

  1. ★ Opening after his intro: "What does a typical NEOGOV seller's day look like right now? Where's the friction that AI could actually take off their plate?"

    • Signals you think in seller-time, not architecture-time. His answer tells you what he actually cares about.

  2. "How's forecasting currently working, and where would AI signal help vs. hurt the accuracy of your commit?"

    • Signals you know Sales VPs live and die by forecast. Also signals you know AI can hurt forecasts if built badly.

  3. "What's your expectation on adoption cadence? Sellers only use tools that make their day easier — how do you think about the transition period when a new AI tool lands?"

    • Signals you know adoption is the real gate, not model accuracy.

  4. ★ The closer (last 2-3 min): "What does 'AI succeeded' look like from your seat — beyond the CRM dashboards? What would you tell your board 6 months in?"

    • Same shape as the Charles closer that worked. Invites him to articulate his real success criteria. Often surfaces the unsaid.

What NOT to do

  • Don't lead with the multi-physics PhD. Wrong audience for the whole conversation.
  • Don't lead with methodology or architecture. Phil doesn't care about PGA / Delta E / Streak Test / VLLMs. Save that for Nick or Charles. Speak business outcomes.
  • Don't over-explain foundation models. He needs "modern AI that helps my sellers." Not gpt-4.1-mini fluency.
  • Don't tell Phil how sales should be done. He knows his motion cold. Ask, don't teach.
  • Don't mention the 1099 / Integrating Consulting angle. Per memory, that's offer-stage only — never in interview rounds.
  • Don't telegraph the Unify timeline. Still private.
  • Don't get technical to prove you're smart. Phil knows you're technical — you wouldn't have made it past Nick and Zach otherwise. He's testing something else.
  • Don't be defensive about "AI is too new for sales." If he brings up skepticism about AI hype, agree with the sensible parts of the concern, then anchor on real production work you've shipped.

Posture

Phil is measuring: would I want this person embedded with my sales leaders?

Warm, curious, business-minded. Listen more than you talk. Ground everything in seller time and outcomes. Treat Phil as a peer, not an evaluator.

Nick and Zach both effectively said yes. Phil's yes closes the door on the technical loop and puts you in offer-conversation territory. This is the gate.

You've got this.
